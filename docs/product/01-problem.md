This file covers the user problem Mimir exists to solve.

Kenyan university students currently use WhatsApp groups as their primary store of school information: timetables (shared as images or spreadsheets), assignment announcements, CAT dates, exam schedules, and administrative deadlines. WhatsApp is also their main social platform, so academic messages compete with chat, memes, and forwarded media. Crucial information gets buried, and recovering it later requires scrolling through long histories or asking a classmate who happened to keep the message.

Timetables suffer the same fate. A student who needs to know their next lecture opens an image from their gallery, finds the right day column, and reads off the venue. The process is manual every time. Students who miss the original broadcast often have no reliable way to recover the information.

Existing time-management apps — myHomework Planner, School Planner — partially address the gap, but the paper identifies three flaws that make them unsuitable for the target audience:

- **Manual setup is heavy.** Every user must enter their own units, lectures, and assignments. Unmotivated students abandon the app mid-setup.
- **Over-flexible UX.** Apps target primary school through university across multiple countries; the resulting UI carries jargon and configuration overhead that does not match a Kenyan university workflow.
- **Paywalled and ad-laden.** Core features sit behind subscriptions Kenyan students are unlikely to pay for.

Mimir's wedge is to be opinionated for Kenyan universities, low-friction to set up, and built around sharing so one student's work serves their classmates. See `docs/product/00-overview.md` for how the product responds, and `docs/product/07-scope.md` for the explicit non-goals.
